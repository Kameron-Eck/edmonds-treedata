# Edmonds Tree Canopy: What the Numbers Are, and What the City Is Doing With Them

A short brief. Part 1 covers the measurements — what exists, how each was made, why they disagree.
Part 2 covers how those numbers moved into city policy. Supporting detail and sources are in
`Edmonds_Report_Dossier.md` in this folder.

---

# PART 1 — THE MEASUREMENTS

## The bottom line

Edmonds has six published canopy percentages. They come from four different organizations, were made
five different ways, and use two different definitions of "the city." Only two of the six are full
measurements of the whole city. Only one has any accuracy check. One is not a measurement at all —
the consultant who produced it calls it an assumption.

These six numbers are routinely lined up as if they showed canopy rising and falling over time. They
cannot be read that way. The differences between them are mostly differences in how they were made.

## The six numbers

| Year | Who | Number | How it was made |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | Davey Resource Group | 32.3% | 1,000 dots on an old Google Earth image, counted by eye |
| 2015 | Davey Resource Group | 30.3% | Software traced canopy on aerial photos, then hand-corrected |
| 2015 | SavATree + Univ. of Vermont | 34.3% | Same idea, but with laser height data added |
| 2020 | SavATree + Univ. of Vermont | 34.6% | Same as above |
| 2021 | Unattributed (NOAA model) | 33.27% | A national NOAA canopy layer, summed up by zoning district |
| "2024" | PlanIT Geo | 32.4% | **Not measured** — an assumption based on 2021 data |

Note the two labelled 2015. Same year, same city, four points apart. That pair is the clearest evidence
that these numbers measure method as much as they measure trees.

## How the measurements were actually made

There are three real ways to count tree canopy, and Edmonds has used all three.

**Counting dots by eye.** Drop a thousand random points on an aerial photo. A person looks at each one
and says "tree" or "not tree." The percentage that land on trees is your canopy estimate. It is cheap
and fast. It is also the crudest method, and Davey said so plainly about their own 2005 number: it is
"not considered as accurate" as their mapped work.

**Tracing canopy from photos.** Software groups pixels by color and texture to find tree crowns, then a
person corrects the mistakes by hand. This is what Davey did for 2015, working from color-infrared
aerial photos flown in August 2015. It produces an actual map, not just an estimate.

**Tracing canopy from photos plus laser.** Same as above, but adds LiDAR — laser pulses that measure how
tall things are. Height data matters because a lawn and a low shrub can look identical to a tree from
directly above, and shadows hide canopy edges. Adding height resolves both. SavATree and the University
of Vermont did this for 2015 and 2020, using 2017 LiDAR.

The remaining two entries are not measurements of Edmonds. The NOAA figure applies a national model to
Edmonds and adds up the result by zoning district. The PlanIT Geo figure is an assumption, discussed in
Part 2.

## Why the numbers disagree

Three reasons, in order of size.

**1. They don't agree on what counts as "Edmonds."** Edmonds is 6,095 acres, of which 402 acres are open
water — Puget Sound inside the city limits. Davey divided canopy by the whole 6,095 acres. SavATree
divided by land only, 5,693 acres. Same trees, different denominator, roughly two percentage points of
difference. Davey's 30.3% becomes 32.4% on SavATree's basis, without a single tree changing.

**2. Laser data finds more canopy than photos alone.** Height information recovers trees that
photo-only methods miss in shadow or confuse with shrubs. Studies using LiDAR should read higher, and
they do.

**3. Only one of them checked its own work.** Davey's 2015 study ran a real accuracy assessment —
random test points, blind classification, a confusion matrix, 89.87% producer's accuracy. No other
Edmonds canopy number has any accuracy figure at all. In the SavATree report the word "accuracy"
appears exactly once, and only in passing.

## The proof: two studies measured the same year

Davey and SavATree both measured **2015**. Since it is the same year and the same city, any difference
between them is error, not tree growth. This is the cleanest test available.

| | Davey 2015 | SavATree 2015 | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| As published | 30.3% | 34.3% | 4.0 points |
| Both on land-only basis | 32.4% | 34.3% | 1.9 points |

**About half the gap is the denominator. The other half is method.** Neither half is trees.

That 4-point spread is larger than every change these reports claim to have detected. SavATree's own
five-year finding was a net gain of 17.6 acres — under half a percentage point, and with no accuracy
figure attached, there is no way to tell it apart from noise.

## What you can and cannot say

**Can say:** Edmonds measured its canopy properly twice, in 2017 and 2022, and got roughly a third of
the city either way. There is no measurement of any kind before 2017 — none in the 1990s, none in the
2000s. The city has never had a public tree inventory.

**Cannot say:** that canopy rose from 30.3% to 34.6%, or fell from 34.6% to 32.4%. Those comparisons
put different methods on the same line. Adjusting the denominators makes the numbers more comparable,
but it does not make them a trend — the sensors, the software, and the rigor still differ.

# PART 2 — HOW THIS ENTERED CITY POLICY

## The short version

The canopy number now being written into Edmonds' tree code is not a measurement. It comes from a
consultant summary that calls it an assumption, it was presented to the Planning Board as a
measurement, and it is now the legal floor in a draft ordinance. The assessment it rests on has never
been published — the conclusion is public, the work behind it is not.

And by the same consultant's own forecast, the city's current planting rate produces a canopy
*decline*, not the increase the goal assumes.

## Where 32.4% came from

It comes from **"A Forecast Analysis of Possible Planting Scenarios," by PlanIT Geo, Inc.**, which
reached the City as Attachment 3 to the **January 28, 2026 Planning Board packet**. A three-page
version dated **July 25, 2024** is published on PlanIT Geo's own website.

The document's own footnote reads:

> **"Edmonds' 2024 canopy cover is an assumption based on 2021 canopy data."**

The published fact sheet also confirms what the figure is divided by: **6,091 total acres, 5,725 land
acres.** And 1,855 ÷ 5,725 = 32.40%. So 32.4% is measured against land only, with open water excluded —
the same convention SavATree used, and the opposite of Davey's. That is worth knowing, because the code
language says only "32.4 percent" and never says which.

## How it became a legal standard

1. **The consultant** reports 32.4% as the city's "current state," footnoted as an assumption drawn from
   2021 data.
2. **The staff report** presents it as a finding: "In Edmonds' 2024 analysis, consultant PlanITGeo
   estimated that existing tree canopy cover is 32.4%... notably lower than Edmonds' 2020 estimated tree
   canopy cover of 34.6 percent."
3. **The Planning Board** turns it into a target: hold 32.4% each year "so that it is 35% by 2036."
4. **Draft ECDC 17.130.000.D** codifies it: "a minimum citywide tree canopy cover goal of 35 percent by
   2036, with a no net loss of 32.4 percent canopy cover based on 2021 imagery."

## Three problems with that chain

**An assumption became a fact.** The consultant declined to call 32.4% a 2024 measurement. The staff
report called it one. City staff acknowledged elsewhere that it is "most likely based on older
imagery."

**The reported decline compares unlike things.** Saying canopy fell from 34.6% to 32.4% sets a LiDAR
measurement against an assumption, from different vendors, in different years, using different methods.
PlanIT Geo's own companion whitepaper concedes the point: "Exact tree canopy percentages vary based on
the data source. Timing and methodologies of studies is not consistent."

**A better 2021 number exists, and it is higher.** A separate analysis using NOAA's 2021 canopy model
puts Edmonds at **33.27%** — same year, nearly a full point above the figure headed into code. The city
adopted the lower one. Because PlanIT Geo's underlying report is unpublished, there is no way to
reconcile the two from public documents. (Caution: this NOAA document has no named author. Confirm who
produced it before citing it publicly.)

## Why 35% is harder than it sounds

Three independent sources agree that Edmonds' canopy sits overwhelmingly on private residential land:

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| NOAA 2021 analysis | Low-Density Residential holds **79%** of all canopy; Public Use 10% |
| PlanIT Geo 2024 | Single Family Residential holds **~80%** of tree cover (1,425 ac) and **~90%** of all plantable area (1,529 ac) |
| SavATree 2022 | Of 1,427 plantable acres, **995 are single-family lawns** |

PlanIT Geo's own scenario table makes the ceiling explicit. It splits plantable land three ways:

| Property type | Plantable acres | Used even in the *lowest* scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Private property | 1,293 | 2% |
| Right-of-way | 197 | 60% |
| **City public property** | **26** | **95%** |

The city owns **26 plantable acres**, and every scenario — including business-as-usual — already uses
95% of them. There is essentially no public land left to plant. The consultant prioritized city-owned
land precisely because the city controls it, and ran out of it immediately.

Reaching 35% therefore depends almost entirely on private property. That is what tree regulation on
private land is for, and it is why the strength of that regulation — not the goal number — decides
whether the goal is reachable.

## The consultant's own forecast says current practice loses canopy

This is the finding most likely to be missed. PlanIT Geo modeled four scenarios out to 2044:

| Scenario | Trees per year | Canopy in 2044 |
|---|---|---|
| **Business as usual** | 170 | **31% — a 1-point loss** |
| Maintain current | 220 | 32% — flat |
| Attainable growth | 325 | 34% |
| Aggressive growth | 425 | 36% |

Under the city's current planting practice, its own consultant projects canopy **falling**, not holding.
Simply staying level requires raising annual planting from 170 trees to 220. No scenario reaches 35%
except "aggressive growth" at 425 trees a year — about two and a half times current practice.

The model assumes **29 acres of canopy lost to development every year**. That assumption, more than the
planting rate, is what the scenarios are working against.

So the adopted goal — 35% by 2036 — sits above the consultant's own "attainable" scenario, on a shorter
timeline (12 years rather than 20), while the city plants at a rate the same consultant projects will
lose ground.

## What is missing

**The 2024 PlanIT Geo canopy assessment itself — the study behind the number going into law — is still
not public.** The planting-scenario summary is available (from the vendor's website, and inside the
Planning Board packet), but that summary is a forecast built *on top of* an assessment. The assessment
is what would contain the method, the imagery, and any accuracy check. It is not on the city website,
not in the city's document server, not in the meeting portal, and did not appear in an archive sweep of
3,673 archived city document URLs.

So the public can see the conclusion but not the work. Nothing available states how the 2021 canopy was
mapped, at what resolution, or with what error rate — for a figure about to become the city's legal
no-net-loss floor. **This is worth a public records request**, and the timing matters given the
adoption schedule.

Also missing: any measured reassessment since 2022 (new LiDAR flights are planned, not done), any study
before 2017, and any public street-tree inventory.

## What to ask for

1. The full 2024 PlanIT Geo canopy assessment — method, imagery dates, accuracy assessment.
2. A stated denominator in the code itself. The consultant's fact sheet shows 32.4% is measured against
   **5,725 land acres, water excluded** — but the draft code says only "32.4 percent." Write the basis
   into the ordinance. Two points ride on it, and a future vendor using the other convention would
   change compliance without a tree moving.
3. An accuracy requirement for any future canopy measurement, so a reported change can be told apart
   from measurement noise.
4. Consistency of method going forward. A canopy goal enforced against numbers produced by rotating
   vendors and methods is not measurable in any meaningful sense.
